How I started: telecalling from my phone

By Sohil Baitha, founder of Switchremote

My first income from remote work came from telecalling. I worked from my phone on two Indian apps, Futwork and SquadStack, and I used both for over a year. I will tell you what it was really like, because most pages about these jobs only show the good side.

  • You have to pass tests first. Both apps make you clear training quizzes and a recorded speaking test before you get any work. On Futwork the final audio test was the hard part, and the result can take about three days. On SquadStack the result came straight away.
  • The pay was lower than the adverts. On Futwork, a full day earned me about ₹550 to ₹600. SquadStack talked about ₹1,000 a day, but ₹300 to ₹500 was closer to the truth. Some days there were not enough leads, and some days people just did not want to talk.
  • Full-time meant full-time. Futwork set a daily target of about five hours of talk time. Reaching it took me seven to eight hours of work. If you missed the target, you were not paid for that day.
  • Part-time was possible on SquadStack. You are paid per call there, so one or two hours of talk time in a day was fine. New callers got only 20 to 30 calls a day at the start.
  • No paid leave. On both apps you earn only for the days and hours you work.

Later I also worked from SquadStack's office in Noida. The work was the same, only on a computer, with a target of eight hours logged in. Sitting next to other callers and mentors made the tests and the daily problems much easier to sort out.

These numbers are from when I did this work, mostly in 2023 and 2024. Projects, targets and pay change often, so check the current details inside each app. When I compared the two, their Play Store ratings were 3.3 and 3.9 out of 5, so read recent reviews before you put your time in.

What the work really is

A company has customers. Those customers get stuck, get confused or get annoyed. You are the person who helps them.

You don't make up what to say. Every project gives you a script and training before your first real call. On Futwork my project was BharatPe, and the script was right there in the app while I was calling. So if you are thinking "I don't know anything, how will I talk", don't worry about that part. They teach it.

One thing to understand before you apply. Support and telecalling are close, but they are not the same. In support, the customer comes to you with a problem. In telecalling, you call people from a list the company gives you, for sales, follow-ups or KYC checks. Both get called "customer support" in job posts, and telecalling comes with targets. Read the description so you know which one you are joining.

The work comes in three forms:

ChatYou type replies in a chat window. Often two or three chats at once.
Email or ticketsYou answer written requests one by one. Less rush, more writing.
PhoneYou talk to customers on calls. This needs a clear voice and a quiet room.

If speaking makes you nervous, look for chat and email roles first. The listing usually says which one it is.

How the pay works on calling apps

This is the part most people get wrong, so read it slowly.

You are paid for talk time, not for keeping the app open. Talk time means the minutes you are actually on a call with a customer. If the app is open for eight hours and you spoke for two, your talk time is two hours. That is why a five-hour target took me seven to eight hours.

Three more things the app taught me:

  • Your first 60 calls are checked. Futwork calls it a quality checkpoint. If you follow the script and don't make mistakes, you continue. If those calls go badly, you go back to training.
  • They watch your average call length. The app shows it as AHT, average handling time. It means how long you keep a customer talking, on average.
  • Payout has a fixed day. On weekly projects the money came into the Futwork wallet every Thursday, and from there I could move it to my bank or UPI. Some projects pay monthly. The project page tells you which one.

Company support jobs are different. They usually pay a monthly salary. Some make you an employee and some make you a contractor, which usually means no paid leave and you handle your own tax. Neither is wrong, but know which one you are signing. When a job on Switchremote shows pay, it is the figure the original listing gives.

How the hiring goes

I will explain it with Futwork, because that is the one I know step by step. Other platforms change the names, but the order is almost the same.

  1. Training and quiz. Inside the app there are seven or eight short modules, and each one ends with a quiz. The answers are in the training document given below the quiz, so read that instead of searching for answers outside.
  2. Grammar and reading test. You choose Hindi or English. I recommend English if you can manage it, because most projects want both languages and you will get more options.
  3. Audio test. They ask simple things: your name, your education, why you want to join. They are checking your voice, your confidence and how clearly you say words.
  4. Placement session. After you clear all three, a mail comes with a Google Meet link. In that meeting there is a short interview, one or two minutes, with basic questions about your experience and languages. Then a project is given to you.
  5. Project training, then real calls. You are trained on that one project first. After that you start calling.

If you think the placement link comes as soon as you make an account, it doesn't. It comes only after the tests are cleared. Many people finish the certificate and then sit waiting, because they never checked their mail. I made a video on exactly that problem.

SquadStack is similar. There is a grammar and speech assessment first. Then a phone interview of 20 to 30 minutes about you and your sales skills. It is a normal call, not a video call. Then training for a few days.

And I will say this clearly: I am not giving you any guarantee of a job. That depends on how you do in the tests and on the calls.

What you need to get started

You do not need a special degree. For calling apps, a smartphone, earphones with a mic and one quiet corner at home are enough. Your voice is the whole job.

Company support jobs ask for more. One example: an Amazon listing for a work-from-home Customer Service Associate in India, which I covered in December 2025, asked for age 18 or above, good written and spoken English, computer skills and Wi-Fi of 100 Mbps download and 10 Mbps upload. It was seasonal work for a few months, five days a week, with shifts that could fall early in the morning, late at night or on weekends. Check the current listing before you apply, because these details change.

So for a company role, expect to need these:

  • Clear written English. Short, correct, friendly sentences.
  • Patience. You will explain the same thing many times a day.
  • A working setup. A laptop or computer, steady internet, a headset if there are calls.
  • Being on time. Support runs on shifts. If you are late, customers wait.

Many remote support jobs serve customers in the United States or Europe. If you live in Asia, that can mean evening or night shifts. Read the working hours and the time zone before you apply. If the listing does not say, ask in the interview.

And don't say "I have no experience" too quickly. Any time you have helped people counts, even if it was not a job. Running a college club page, helping at a family shop, tutoring, telecalling. Put these on your resume and say what you did.

How to apply for a company support job

  1. Find the right listings. On Switchremote, pick where you live and search for "support". See customer support jobs now.
  2. Read the whole listing. Check the country, the hours, chat or phone, and what they ask for.
  3. Fix your resume for this job. Put anything about helping people, writing and being reliable near the top. One page is enough.
  4. Apply on the company's own page. Answer every question in full sentences. Many companies treat these answers as your first writing test.
  5. Expect a small test. Often they send two or three sample customer messages and ask you to reply. Be clear, be kind, and solve the problem in the first reply if you can.
  6. Apply to several. Most freshers apply to many jobs before one says yes. That is normal and it is not about you.

Questions you will probably be asked

  • "Tell me about a time you helped someone who was upset." Use a real story. What was wrong, what you did, how it ended.
  • "A customer is angry and it is not your fault. What do you do?" Listen first, say sorry for the trouble, fix what you can, and tell them clearly what happens next.
  • "What would you do if you did not know the answer?" Say you would check the help guide, then ask a teammate, and tell the customer you are finding out instead of guessing.
  • "Can you work these hours?" Be honest. Saying yes to a night shift you cannot keep ends badly for everyone.

How a fake offer works

Fake offers use "customer support" and "chat support" as bait, because so many freshers are looking for it. The pattern is almost always the same:

  1. A message comes on WhatsApp or Telegram. Easy work, good money every day, no interview.
  2. The work is something like liking videos or writing hotel reviews.
  3. They really pay you the first small amount. Now you trust them.
  4. Then they say: pay a small amount to unlock bigger tasks. You pay, and they return a little more than you paid.
  5. The amount keeps growing. One day you send a big amount, and they block you.

The first payment is not your salary. It is how they win your trust. So keep these rules:

  • Never pay to get work. Not for registration, not for training, not for a laptop or a kit.
  • Never let anyone use your bank account. If someone offers a commission to receive money and send it forward, say no.
  • Don't send ID, bank or card details before there is a written offer from a real company.
  • Don't install an app from a link. If it is not on the Play Store or App Store, leave it.

Where I would start

If I was starting again today with only a phone, I would do it in this order:

  1. Fix the basics first: earphones with a mic, and one quiet corner at home.
  2. Pick one platform and finish every test on it. Half-done accounts on five apps will get you nothing.
  3. Give the tests honestly and slowly. Read the training document before the quiz.
  4. Check your mail every day after you clear them. That is where the next step comes.
  5. On your first project, follow the script exactly for the first 60 calls. Speed comes later.

It is a simple process. Nothing in it is complicated, but it needs a few days of patience before the first payment comes. And it is a starting point, not a dead end. People move from support into quality checking, team lead, customer success and operations roles.
